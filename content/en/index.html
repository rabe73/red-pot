Red Pot

Shopping lists, pantry and week planning for several households. Kept by voice, working offline, with nobody else's servers involved — and a food model that understands how cooking actually works.

Home, holiday flat, camper van

Each place has its own list, its own pantry and its own people. Red Pot keeps them apart and still together. Everyone who belongs sees the same list as it changes — over iCloud, with no account here. Several households cost nothing. An app that pins you to a single list cannot really be tried at all.

Speak instead of type

"Butter is running low" is enough. In the supermarket, one-handed, on a bad signal — checking off works offline, because that is where it has to work. Typing loses you nothing: voice is a shortcut, not a requirement.

A pantry that knows its dates

Sorted by estimated time remaining, so what has to go first is at the top. The freezer uses honest figures rather than optimism: three months for cooked dishes, six for braised red cabbage and compote — and potato soup is never offered for freezing at all, because it turns mealy.

The food model

This is where the work went. Red Pot does not store recipes; it stores components — a sauce, a side, a piece of meat, each with its method, flavour profile, equipment needs and scaling behaviour. A dish happens when components meet on a plate. It sounds like a detour and it solves four problems at once:

  • The plate is never empty. Missing an ingredient replaces one component instead of discarding the whole recipe.
  • Quantities are actually right. Every ingredient scales by its own rule. Rice doubles with the portions, oil in the pan does not, garlic certainly does not, and eggs only come whole. That is why a recipe for six tastes right for six.
  • Batch cooking is built in. Beef roulades for one meal are too much work; for six portions they are twelve minutes a meal. Anything worth freezing is tracked as portions, with a way to reheat it — usually a pot with a splash of liquid, but cooked pasta needs a microwave, because a pot turns it to mush.
  • The app can genuinely learn. 260 main meals a year are never enough to estimate a preference for individual dishes. Across attributes — sour, heavy, hot, pan-fried — they are plenty. So Red Pot learns about attributes rather than about dishes.

Planning the way people plan

Not all at once. First a look at the week: above 30 °C, or a sudden drop — that is all the weather says, but it says it. Then you fix the main component of each day, together, in conversation. Only once that frame stands do sides, sauces and side meals follow.

Elaborate dishes drift to the weekend, judged by total time rather than hands-on time. A real beef stock is 25 minutes of work and four and a half hours on the stove; those four and a half hours are why it is not a Tuesday.

Discover

A button that deliberately overrides your own caution and offers three deliberately unlike dishes you have never had — different kind of dish, different headline ingredient, different cuisine. Someone who describes themselves as conservative otherwise never sees anything new, and so never finds out what they might like.

What is hard stays hard. Intolerances and real dislikes are filters, not weights — and they apply to whoever is eating that evening. If one person dislikes fish but is eating at the office that day, the others can have fish.

And what worked, stays

A meal that fit can be kept and named. It is stored as its components rather than as text — so next time it rescales to however many are eating, checks itself against the current pantry, and improves whenever one of its components does.

What Red Pot does not do

It collects nothing. There is no Red Pot server holding anything about you, because there is no Red Pot server at all. The recipe components ship with the app; nothing is searched for on the web and nothing is sent to a language model in the cloud. Details under Privacy.
