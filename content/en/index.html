Red Pot

Your pantry, your week's plan and your shopping list are the same thing. Red Pot works them into each other — you only say what is running low and who is eating.

The list was never the problem

Everyone can keep a list. And still, at ten to seven, somebody is standing in front of a full fridge with no idea what to make.

That is not a lack of discipline. Three things that belong together are kept apart: what you have, what you cook, what you need to buy. Translating between them is done by a person — tired, in the evening, from memory. And there are good apps for each third:

  • Shopping lists do not know what is already in your kitchen.
  • Recipe apps hand you a recipe that is missing an ingredient.
  • Pantry apps want a perfect inventory — after a fortnight you stop.

Red Pot is where the three become one: the pantry falls out of shopping and cooking, the week's plan out of the pantry, the shopping list out of the plan. Nobody translates any more.

In passing, not as a project

No account, no setup wizard, no scanning marathon. “Butter is running low”, said or typed on the way past, and that is it. One-handed in the supermarket, signal or no signal.

What has to go first is simply at the top of the list — an order, not a warning. And fresh food is not bought too early: every cooking day has an earliest and a last shopping day, and when holidays shut the shops for three days running, the list says so in advance.

Your week, not the app's

A Tuesday is not a Sunday. How much time a weekday has, when the warm meal is, who is at the table — that belongs to the household, not to the planner. Elaborate food lands where there is time for it; midweek, what is ready in half an hour.

And cook once, eat twice, if you like: a roast that only makes sense at six portions comes with a plan for the rest — freezer, reheat day, as big as your freezer happens to be.

One evening a week buys nothing

Food is rarely thrown away out of carelessness, but as the remainder of the packet: you need a third of a bunch of parsley, and the bunch only comes whole.

So any meal in the week may be a Joker. Nothing is bought for it; it is built on the day out of what is in the house and going off soon — usually a pan, a soup, a bake, where the method carries the dish rather than any one ingredient.

A pantry is never accurate to the gram anyway. So the app does not ask how much is left — it shows what the recipe needs for the people eating. If something is short, a line goes on the list. You decide at the stove.

Changing habits without resolving to

Resolving to eat more vegetables or to cook more seasonally fails at the same point every time: it demands a decision exactly when you have no energy for one.

Red Pot turns that around: it does not change what you resolve, it changes what gets put in front of you. Eat seasonally? Say it once. Something new? One button, three dishes with nothing in common that you have never had.

It learns from what happens — cooked, swapped, left out — not from a questionnaire. Where tastes pull apart, everyone gets their turn over the weeks. Intolerances and real dislikes are filters rather than suggestions, and apply to whoever is actually eating. A vegetarian table gets the same range, not the short menu.

For more than one home

Home, the holiday flat, the camper van: different places, different pantries, different people. Red Pot keeps them apart and still together — everyone who belongs sees the same list as it changes. Each place has its own kitchen: two burners and no oven, so nothing baked.

Guests are the same day with more people: courses and tasks — drinks, music, tables — sit there as cards, and everyone claims what they claim. What the guests do not eat counts in the suggestions, without anyone learning whose.

Why any of this works

Because Red Pot does not store recipes. It stores parts: a sauce, a side, a piece of meat. A dish only exists once they meet.

So a missing ingredient swaps out a part instead of cancelling the evening, the quantities are right for six as well as for two, and the app can learn anything about you at all: not about individual dishes — nobody eats the same thing often enough — but about qualities like rich, sharp or light.

Why today's dish is there, the app says in one sentence. And when there is nothing to explain, it says that too.

What Red Pot does not do

It collects nothing. There is no Red Pot server holding anything about you, because there is no Red Pot server at all: everything stays in your iCloud and on your devices. And you can get back out — the export is a readable file, worth something even without this app. Details under Privacy.
