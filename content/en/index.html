Red Pot

Keeping track of food has never been this simple or this natural — and changing something about how you eat has never been this easy.

The list was never the problem

Everyone can keep a list. And still there is a second jar of mustard in the cupboard, something at the back of the fridge nobody wants to touch any more, and at ten to seven somebody standing in front of a full fridge with no idea what to make.

That is not a lack of discipline. It is that three things which belong together are kept apart: what you have, what you cook and what you need to buy. Translating between them is done by a person — tired, in the evening, from memory.

Why not another app

There are good apps for each third of that.

  • Shopping lists are lists. They do not know what food is, let alone what is already in your kitchen.
  • Recipe apps hand you a recipe you cannot cook, because one ingredient is missing. You adapt to the app, not the other way round.
  • Pantry apps ask you to scan everything and keep the inventory perfect. After a fortnight you stop.
  • Nutrition apps count and admonish. Dinner still does not cook itself.

Red Pot is none of those separately. It is where the three become one thing: the pantry falls out of shopping and cooking, the week's plan falls out of the pantry, and the shopping list falls out of the plan. Nobody translates any more.

Let us start simply

First the part that pays off immediately: describe the household once, know what is in the cupboards, and a shopping list that takes you at your word. That is a complete app, it is free, and it comes first.

You could stay there for years without ever touching a week plan. It is the work no other shopping list does, because they all stop at the list instead of starting at the pantry.

In passing, not as a project

"Butter is running low", said or typed on the way past, and that is it. No account, no setup wizard, no scanning marathon to begin with. The way in does not matter — what matters is that the line gets written at the moment somebody notices it.

Ticking off in the supermarket takes one hand, and no signal — or the watch, when the phone is to stay in your pocket.

And because the app knows how long things keep, what has to go first is simply at the top — as an order, not as a warning.

One day a week is shopping day

Nobody keeps a pantry up to date continuously, so Red Pot does not ask you to. There is one day, and on that day the app walks once through what might be missing — the staples, the things this household simply wants to always have. Flour, mustard, washing-up liquid, the dog food. Whatever is missing goes on the list.

That is the whole inventory: once a week, a handful of lines, done before the coffee goes cold. An article this household never needs is gone with one swipe and never asks again.

For more than one home

Home, the holiday flat, the camper van: different places, different pantries, different people. Red Pot keeps them apart and still together, and everyone who belongs sees the same list as it changes.

You invite whoever is part of it — your flatmate, your partner, the children. No Red Pot account is needed for that; it runs over iCloud, and being invited is all it takes.

On the devices you already have

iPhone, iPad, Mac and Apple Watch — all four from the start, one app rather than a separate product per device. The Mac is where you set the household up and build the base equipment, the iPhone is where daily life happens, and the watch is there for the shop: the list, the amount, and which household the line belongs to. Nobody needs more than that in an aisle.

The watch keeps a cache of its own. Ticking off therefore works where the network stops — in the supermarket basement as much as at the holiday house.

When the app should take the week off your hands

The slightly fuller description of the household — how the week looks, what the kitchen can do, who does not eat what — becomes relevant exactly when you want the app to take the week planning off your hands as well. You can give all of it from the start. Here is where it has consequences.

Because now the circle closes: the pantry says what has to go. The plan turns it into dinner. Dinner writes the next shopping list. The inventory corrects the pantry. And round again.

That comes later and it costs something, and both for the same reason: whether a shopping list is right takes a second to see. Whether a week plan is good takes weeks at real tables. Shipping it before it is right would break the very thing it exists for.

What the household hasStock, list, shopping day. The same few motions every week, and nobody has to remember them.

What the household eatsThat turns into dinner, and dinner writes the next list. Every week as well.

Who the household invitesNot routine but occasions: different friends each time, and each of them brings something to it.

One evening a week buys nothing

Food is rarely thrown away out of carelessness. What gets thrown away is the remainder of the packet: you need a third of a bunch of parsley, and the bunch only comes whole.

So any meal in the week may be a Joker. Nothing is bought for it; it is built on the day itself out of what is in the house and going off soon — usually a pan, a soup, a bake, because there the method carries the dish rather than any one ingredient.

Here the one reaches into the other: that the cream runs out on Friday has been in the pantry for weeks. What becomes of it on Friday is the week plan's decision.

A pantry is never accurate to the gram, and it does not need to be. So the app does not quiz you on what is left — it shows what the recipe needs for the people eating. If that is not enough, a line goes on the list. You decide at the stove.

Changing habits without resolving to

Resolving to eat more vegetables, to cook more seasonally, or to finally try something new fails at the same point every time: it demands a decision exactly when you have no energy for one.

Red Pot turns that around. It does not change what you resolve; it changes what gets put in front of you. Want to eat seasonally? Say so once, and it is what you are offered from then on, instead of something to remember every Thursday. Want to try something new? One button, three dishes that deliberately have nothing to do with each other and that you have never had.

It learns from what actually happens — what gets cooked, swapped, left out, repeated. Not from a questionnaire. And what is out of the question stays out of the question: intolerances and real dislikes are filters rather than suggestions, applied to whoever is actually eating that evening.

Every kitchen is different

Each place has its own kitchen, too. In the van that is two burners and no oven — so nothing baked is ever suggested there, not merely seldom: what a kitchen can do is a fact, not a preference. You do not list appliances for it — "camper", "no oven", "with a grill" are ready-made profiles to pick from.

The same goes for time. A Tuesday with twenty minutes in it does not get a dish that needs ninety. Cook once a week for two evenings and you get a plan where that works out — and only as much cooked ahead as the freezer holds. It need not be a large one: for two people the plan never lays down more than a dozen cooked portions anyway.

Why any of this works

Because Red Pot does not store recipes. It stores parts: a sauce, a side, a piece of meat. A dish only exists once they meet.

So a missing ingredient swaps out one part instead of cancelling the evening. So the quantities are right for six as well as for two. And so the app can learn anything about you at all: about individual dishes it never could — nobody eats the same thing often enough — but about qualities like rich, sharp, fried or light, it easily can.

And later

In time, host cooking events with friends — events the guests contribute to as well.

What Red Pot does not do

It collects nothing. There is no Red Pot server holding anything about you, because there is no Red Pot server at all. Everything stays in your own iCloud and on your devices. Details under Privacy.
